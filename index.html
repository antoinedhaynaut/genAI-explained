Part 0 / The map

ML vs GenAI: where does this all fit?

"AI" gets used for everything, so let's place the terms first. Machine Learning (ML) is the broad discipline: instead of programming explicit rules, you show a program lots of examples and let it learn the pattern itself. ML has been in production in IT for 15+ years — spam filters, anomaly detection on your network, capacity forecasting, "users like you also opened this ticket." Classic ML recognizes, classifies, and predicts: it takes data in and outputs a label, a score, or a number.

GenAI (Generative AI) is a recent branch of ML where the model's output is not a label but new content — text, code, images, audio. LLMs are the text-generating flavor of GenAI, and they're what this guide focuses on. Same family, different job: classic ML tells you "this transaction looks fraudulent (94%)"; GenAI writes the fraud-investigation summary.

AnalogyClassic ML is a seasoned inspector: show it a thousand photos of defective parts and it will spot the next defect instantly — but it can only ever say "defect / no defect." GenAI is a craftsman: it studied the same photos and can now produce a report, a repair procedure, or a picture of what the fixed part should look like.
Why it matters in practiceDifferent problems, different tools. Forecasting disk usage or scoring alert severity? That's classic ML — cheaper, faster, deterministic, and often the better choice. Summarizing incidents, drafting responses, answering questions over documentation? That's GenAI. A common architecture mistake is using an expensive LLM for a job a small ML model solves better.
Part 1 / The engine

What a Large Language Model actually is

A LLM (Large Language Model) is a program that has read a colossal amount of text — most of the public internet, books, code, documentation — and from all of it learned one skill extremely well: given a sequence of words, predict what comes next. That's it. There is no database of facts inside, no reasoning engine bolted on. Everything else — answering questions, writing code, summarizing reports — emerges from doing that one prediction, billions of times, very well.

AnalogyIt's a much more powerful autocomplete. Your phone predicts the next word from your last texts; an LLM predicts it from having read more text than a human could in ten thousand lifetimes. At that scale, "predicting the next word" starts to look a lot like understanding.

Training vs inference: the model doesn't learn from you

A model's life has two phases. Training is when it learns: months of processing massive datasets on thousands of GPUs, costing tens of millions. It happens once (per version), in the lab. Inference is when it's used: every prompt you send runs the frozen, finished model. Nothing you type changes the model. Your chats feel like the AI "remembers" only because the conversation stays on the whiteboard (see context window, below) — close the session and, from the model's point of view, you never existed.

AnalogyTraining is medical school; inference is seeing patients. The doctor doesn't rewire their brain after each consultation — they apply what they already learned. Notes about you live in your patient file (the context), not in the doctor's neurons.
Why it matters in practiceThis answers the #1 question colleagues will ask: "Does it learn from our data?" By default, no — inference doesn't train the model. The real questions are contractual: does the vendor log your prompts, and do they reuse them to train future versions? Enterprise plans typically guarantee they don't; consumer free tiers may not. Check the data-usage terms, not the technology.
Nexthink exampleThis is exactly what the Nexthink Global AI Hub documents: the platform's AI features use off-the-shelf LLMs via AWS Bedrock, Nexthink does not fine-tune them, and customer data is never used to train models. The features run inference on your telemetry; the models themselves stay frozen. When a customer asks "will our data end up in the model?", the AI Hub is the public, verifiable answer.

Tokens: the LEGO bricks of language

Models don't work with words — they work with tokens: chunks of text of roughly 3–4 characters. "Server" is one token; "virtualization" might be three ("virtual", "iz", "ation"). Everything you send and receive is converted to tokens, and pricing, speed and limits are all measured in them. A simple rule: 1,000 tokens ≈ 750 English words.

The context window: the model's whiteboard

The context window is how many tokens the model can "see" at once — your prompt, the conversation so far, any documents you pasted, plus its own answer in progress. Modern models handle 200K+ tokens (a few hundred pages).

AnalogyThink of a whiteboard in a meeting room. Everything on the board is available to everyone; anything that gets wiped off is simply gone. The model has no memory outside the whiteboard — when a conversation "forgets" something from an hour ago, it scrolled off the board.
Nexthink exampleWorkspace (powered by Nexthink Assist) is a context window in action: it keeps track of the ongoing conversation so you can refine an investigation with follow-up questions — "now only Windows 11 devices", "break it down by location" — without restarting. The conversation thread is the whiteboard.

Inside the model: the neural network

So what actually does the predicting? A neural network: a very large web of simple mathematical units, loosely inspired by how neurons connect in a brain. Each unit takes numbers in, multiplies them by its own weights, and passes a number on to the next layer. Stack hundreds of these layers, connect billions of units, and the whole network becomes a giant function: tokens go in one end, and a score for every possible next token comes out the other.

Those billions of weights are the model — they are the only thing that changes during training. At the start they are random and the network outputs nonsense. It is then shown text with the last word hidden, asked to guess it, and every time it is wrong the weights are nudged slightly so the network would be a little less wrong next time. Repeat across trillions of examples and the weights slowly settle into values that encode grammar, facts, and patterns of reasoning — not stored as a lookup table anywhere, but spread across the whole network. This is why an LLM is often called a "black box": we know the weights, but no one can point to where a specific fact "lives."

AnalogyPicture the mixing desk in a recording studio, but with billions of sliders instead of a hundred. Training is the long process of adjusting every slider until the sound coming out is right. Once set, the sliders are frozen — using the model just runs signal through that fixed configuration. Nobody can say "this one slider is the guitar"; the sound comes from all of them together.

Putting it together: predicting one token at a time

Now the full picture. Your text becomes tokens, the tokens run through the neural network, and out comes a probability score for every token that could come next. The model picks one, adds it to the text, and runs the entire thing again to get the following token. It repeats this loop — predict, append, repeat — until the answer is complete. Why it matters in practiceTwo consequences worth carrying into a customer conversation. First, an answer is built one token at a time with no plan of the whole sentence — which is why models can start confidently and then drift. Second, because each token is chosen from a probability, the same prompt can give different answers (the next concept below). The fluency is real; the certainty is not guaranteed.

Hallucinations: confident nonsense

Because the model generates statistically plausible text rather than looking facts up, it will sometimes produce answers that are fluent, confident, and wrong. That's a hallucination. It's not a bug being fixed next release — it's a direct consequence of how prediction works. The mitigations are exactly what the rest of this guide covers: give the model real data (RAG) and real tools (agents) so it doesn't have to improvise.

Watch outAn LLM never says "I'm 40% sure" on its own. A hallucinated ITIL process reference reads exactly as confidently as a real one. Trust, but verify — especially for numbers, names, and citations.
Nexthink exampleThis is why Nexthink marks every AI-generated insight with the ✦ sparkles icon and explicitly recommends validating results before acting on them. Labeling AI output and keeping a human check in the loop isn't a disclaimer — it's the standard, responsible way to ship GenAI in an enterprise product.

Deterministic vs probabilistic: same prompt, different answer

Everything you've administered until now is deterministic: same input, same output, every time. Run the script twice, get the same result twice — and if you don't, it's a bug. An LLM is probabilistic: at every step it holds a distribution of candidate next tokens (as in the demo at the top) and picks among the likely ones with a dose of randomness. Ask the same question twice and you'll get two differently-worded — sometimes differently-structured — answers. That's not a malfunction; it's the design.

The dose of randomness is a setting called temperature. Low temperature (near 0) makes the model almost always pick the top candidate: consistent, repeatable, a bit flat — what you want for classification, extraction, or code. High temperature lets it explore less likely tokens: more varied and creative, but less predictable — good for brainstorming and writing.

AnalogyA deterministic system is a vending machine: B4 gives you the same snack every single time. An LLM is a good chef: order the same dish twice and it will be recognizably the same dish, but never molecule-for-molecule identical. Temperature is how much you let the chef improvise.
Why it matters in practiceYou can't test GenAI like a script — asserting on exact output will fail. You evaluate it like you'd evaluate a person's work: on samples, against criteria ("is it accurate? grounded? in policy?"). And for anything compliance-critical, you pin the temperature down and add checks around the output.

Fine-tuning: onboarding the new hire

Fine-tuning means taking a trained model and training it a bit more on your own examples — support tickets, your writing style, your product docs — so the behavior becomes second nature. It changes the model itself, which is powerful but heavy. In practice, most companies get further with a good prompt plus RAG (next part) and only fine-tune when they need consistent style or a specialized domain at scale.

AnalogyRAG is handing a smart consultant your binder of documentation before the meeting. Fine-tuning is sending them to a three-week internal training. The consultant with the binder is cheaper, faster to update, and usually good enough.

Multimodal: beyond text

Early LLMs only handled text. A multimodal model also takes images, audio, video, or documents as input — and increasingly produces them as output. Under the hood it's the same trick: a screenshot is converted into tokens the model can process alongside your words. Practically, this is huge for IT: paste a screenshot of an error dialog, a photo of a rack, or an architecture diagram, and ask questions about it directly — no transcribing.

AnalogyA text-only model is a brilliant colleague you can only reach by chat. A multimodal one is on a video call: you can share your screen and just say "look at this."
Nexthink exampleWorkspace accepts image attachments in a conversation: paste a screenshot of an error or a dashboard, and Assist uses it as context for its answer — the multimodal "screen-share" pattern, live in the product.

Open-weight vs proprietary: the build-vs-buy of AI

A proprietary model (GPT, Claude, Gemini) is used through the vendor's API or apps: state-of-the-art quality, zero infrastructure, but your prompts transit the vendor's cloud and you pay per token. An open-weight model (Llama, Mistral, Qwen…) can be downloaded and run on your own servers: full data control, no per-token fees, works air-gapped — but you own the GPUs, the ops, the updates, and quality typically trails the frontier models. Most enterprises end up hybrid: proprietary via API for general use, open-weight self-hosted where data can't leave.

AnalogyIt's SaaS vs self-hosted, exactly like the choice you've already made for email, CRM or monitoring. Same trade-off triangle: convenience and cutting-edge features vs control, sovereignty and predictable cost.
Nexthink exampleThere's a third way, and Nexthink uses it: consuming models through a managed cloud service (AWS Bedrock) running inside Nexthink's own AWS environment, in the customer's geographic region. You get frontier-class models and data residency — a pattern worth knowing when customers raise sovereignty concerns.
Part 2 / Grounding it in your data

Embeddings, vector databases & RAG

Out of the box, an LLM knows nothing about your company: not your runbooks, not your pricing, not last Tuesday's incident. And its knowledge stops at its training cutoff date. The fix is not to retrain the model — it's to fetch the right documents at question time and put them on the whiteboard. That pattern is called RAG: Retrieval-Augmented Generation.

Embeddings: GPS coordinates for meaning

To fetch "the right documents," you need search that understands meaning, not just keywords. An embedding converts a piece of text into a long list of numbers (a vector) that captures what it's about. Texts with similar meaning land close together in that number space: "the laptop won't boot" ends up near "PC fails to start", even though they share almost no words.

Vector database: a library sorted by meaning

A vector database stores those embeddings and answers one question fast: "here's a vector — find the stored vectors closest to it." Chunk your documentation, embed each chunk, store the vectors, and you can retrieve the most relevant paragraphs for any question in milliseconds.

AnalogyA normal database is a library sorted alphabetically — great if you know the exact title. A vector database is a library where books about similar topics physically sit next to each other, so you walk to "printer problems" and every relevant book is on that shelf, whatever its title.

Putting it together: RAG

Why it matters: the model answers from your current documents, can cite its sources, and updating knowledge means updating a document — not retraining a model. This is the single most common pattern in enterprise GenAI today, and it's what most "chat with your data" products are underneath.

Nexthink exampleAsk Workspace a question and Assist retrieves before it answers: it queries Nexthink documentation, Infinity configuration objects (dashboards, library packs) and live data via NQL, then composes a grounded response with links to the underlying investigations. And AI Insights follows the same grounding principle with telemetry instead of documents: the impact analysis on an alert or the tooltip in Network View is generated from real collected data placed in the model's context — which is why it can explain root cause instead of guessing.
Part 3 / From answering to acting

Agentic AI, MCP & Agent2Agent

Agentic: giving the model hands

A plain LLM can only talk. An agent is an LLM placed inside a loop and given tools — search the web, query a database, call an API, run code, send an email. The loop looks like: think → pick a tool → use it → look at the result → decide the next step → repeat until done. "Agentic" describes systems built this way: the model doesn't just answer, it plans and executes multi-step work, adjusting as results come in.

AnalogyA plain LLM is a brilliant consultant on the phone: great advice, but they can't touch anything. An agent is the same consultant sitting at a workstation with your apps open — they can check the ticket queue, run the query, and draft the reply themselves.
Nexthink exampleSpark is this loop shipped as a product: a personal IT agent that understands an employee's issue in natural language, reasons over live device context from Infinity, pulls from knowledge sources (KB articles, past ticket insights, the automation catalog), then acts — proposing manual steps or running IT-approved remediations, and escalating to the service desk with full context when it can't resolve. Note the design choices that make an enterprise agent trustworthy: every action is pre-approved by IT, governed by policy guardrails, and fully logged. Autonomy without a black box.

MCP: one standard to connect AI to your systems

Historically, every tool connection was a custom integration: your CRM one way, your ticketing system another, each AI vendor requiring its own connector. MCP (Model Context Protocol) is an open standard that fixes this. A system exposes its capabilities once as an "MCP server" ("I can search tickets, create tickets, read a knowledge base"), and any MCP-compatible AI can plug in and use them — no custom integration code for every AI-tool pairing.

Agent2Agent (A2A): agents talking to each other

MCP connects an agent to tools. Agent2Agent (A2A) is a protocol for agents to talk to other agents — discover what another agent can do, hand off a task, and get results back, even across vendors and companies. Picture a procurement agent asking a supplier's agent for a quote, or an IT agent delegating a network diagnosis to a specialized network agent.

AnalogyMCP gives one employee access to the tools on their desk. A2A is the company org chart and email system: it lets specialized colleagues find each other, delegate, and collaborate on a job too big for one person.
The thread to rememberAn LLM only predicts text. Everything trustworthy about modern AI — real data (RAG), real actions (agents, MCP), real collaboration (A2A) — comes from the system built around that predictor.
Part 4 / In the backend

Two prompts, explained step by step

Same chat box, radically different machinery behind it. Here's what actually runs in the backend for a simple prompt versus an agentic one.

Example 1 — Pure LLM (no tools, no data)

You type"Write a short apology email to a customer whose migration was delayed by two days."
1

Tokenization

Your sentence is split into ~20 tokens and converted to numbers. A hidden system prompt (the assistant's standing instructions: tone, rules, format) is placed on the whiteboard before your message.

2

One giant prediction pass

The model computes probabilities for the first token of the reply. From everything it has read, after an instruction like this, replies overwhelmingly start with something like Subject: or Dear.

3

Loop, one token at a time

The chosen token is appended to the whiteboard, and the model predicts the next one. Repeat a few hundred times. That's why answers stream word by word — you're literally watching the loop run.

4

Done

The model emits a special "stop" token. Total time: 2–5 seconds. Nothing was looked up. The email is good because millions of apology emails shaped the model's sense of what one looks like.

Example 2 — Agentic + RAG (tools and your data)

You type"How many P1 incidents did we have last month, and what does our runbook say about the most frequent root cause?"
1

Plan

The LLM reads the question plus a list of tools it's been given (via MCP, for example: query_itsm, search_runbooks). It decides this needs two steps and can't be answered from memory.

2

Tool call #1 — live data

It emits a structured request: query_itsm(priority="P1", period="last_month"). The platform executes it against the real ITSM system and pastes the result — 17 incidents, top cause: expired certificates (6) — onto the whiteboard.

3

Tool call #2 — RAG

Now it calls search_runbooks("expired certificate renewal procedure"). Behind that tool: the query is embedded, the vector DB returns the 3 closest runbook chunks, and those paragraphs land on the whiteboard too.

4

Verify & synthesize

The model now answers using only what's on the whiteboard: "17 P1s; most frequent cause was expired certificates (6 cases); the runbook prescribes… [source: cert-renewal-runbook §3]." The numbers are real because they were fetched, not remembered.

5

The difference

Ask Example 1's setup this question and it will hallucinate a plausible number. Ask Example 2's and it retrieves the truth. Same model — the architecture around it makes it trustworthy.
